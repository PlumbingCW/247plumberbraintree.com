Winter in Randolph: frozen pipes and the 48 inch frost line

The nearest NOAA station we read is the Blue Hill Observatory area in Milton, and its 1991 to 2020 normals count about 121 days a year with lows at or below freezing. January alone averages 27.5 such days. Randolph is not measured there, so treat those as indicative. The takeaway is that pipes in exterior walls, unheated garages and crawlspaces face a long freezing season.

The Massachusetts residential code sets a frost line depth of 48 inches, and the plumbing code requires protection for exposed pipes that could freeze. If a faucet slows to a trickle on a very cold morning, the line is probably frozen but not yet burst. Open the faucet, warm the wall with a hair dryer on low and never use an open flame. If a pipe has already split, you need burst pipe repair right away.

Spring thaw and heavy rain

The Blue Hill normals show about 53.9 inches of precipitation and 69.7 inches of snowfall in an average year. When that snow melts onto saturated ground, basements with a weak sump pump are the first to show it. For you, that means testing the pump in March, not in the middle of a storm.

Pour water into the pit until the float rises and confirm the pump starts and clears the water. A discharge line must carry water outdoors and away from the foundation. If the pump is old, short-cycling or silent, a sump pump replacement with a battery backup is the more dependable choice.

Summer: hose bibs, irrigation and the water bill

Summer rarely brings emergencies, but it brings quiet leaks. A hose bib that drips behind a shrub, an irrigation valve stuck open or a toilet that runs all night can all send the water bill higher. A simple test is to shut off every fixture and watch the meter. If it still turns, water is going somewhere.

That is when a leak detection visit helps. Pressure tests and listening equipment find a leak without opening every wall. We would rather locate a buried line precisely than dig a trench on a guess.

Fall: water heaters and the first cold snap

October is when old water heaters announce themselves. A rumbling tank, rust-colored hot water or moisture at the base all point to a unit near the end. Replacing a tank by choice in October beats an emergency swap in January, and we can often book it as a planned visit.

Under state rule 248 CMR 3.05, replacing a water heater needs a permit, and permits go to licensed individuals. Randolph's Inspectional Services is at Town Hall, 41 South Main Street, second floor, with the main line at 781-961-0921. The town lists the plumbing inspector's phone as 781-961-0920 for Monday, Wednesday and Thursday, and asks that you call before 9 am. We handle that contact for water heater replacement jobs.

Randolph's water: the shared Great Pond system

Randolph is served by the Randolph-Holbrook Joint Water System. Water is produced at the Great Pond Water Treatment Plant in Randolph from Lower Great Pond, Upper Great Pond, Richardi Reservoir and the Farm River. Braintree's own report describes the same Great Pond reservoir system as its main source, so the three towns share a water origin.

Holbrook's 2025 report lists hardness for the shared system at 56 mg/L from a single sample, which is about 3.3 grains per gallon by simple arithmetic. Braintree's 2024 report prints 47.8 mg/L. Water in this range is on the soft side, so mineral scale builds slowly in a water heater, and age and anode condition matter more than a softener.

Holbrook's report also says a new treatment plant is scheduled to go into service in 2026. If your water looks cloudy or rust-colored after a change, run the cold tap for a few minutes, and if it persists, call Randolph's water division at 781-961-0942.

Crawford Square, West Village, Tower Hill and the housing mix

The town's 2013 Preservation Plan names Crawford Square as the commercial and institutional center, with early residential concentrations in West Village and Tower Hill. Stetson Hall, built in 1842, is a landmark of that older core. Buildings in those older areas may hold older drains and original shutoff valves, so tell us before we cut into a wall.

Census survey estimates put Randolph's median year built at 1966. About 14 percent of units date from 1939 or earlier, 23 percent from 1940 to 1959, and 32 percent from 1960 to 1979. About 71 percent are owner-occupied and 73 percent are single-unit. Homes from the 1960s and 1970s are now 50 to 60 years old, which is old enough for original supply stops and fill valves to seize.

We also serve the neighbors on either side. See how we handle Holbrook, which buys its water from the same system, and Quincy. For a recurring slow drain anywhere in Randolph, drain cleaning with a camera look is the first step.
