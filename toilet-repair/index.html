Toilets look simple, but each symptom points to a specific part. Match the symptom to the cause and the repair is usually short.

What to do when the toilet is overflowing right now

Do not flush again. Reaching for the plunger can wait until the water stops rising.

  • Turn the shutoff valve behind or beside the toilet clockwise until it stops.
  • Take off the tank lid and push the flapper down to stop more water entering the bowl.
  • Put towels around the base and keep people off the wet floor.
  • If water is coming out of other fixtures too, shut the main valve and call for a drain clearing.

A leak at the base of the toilet

A toilet sits on a wax ring (or a rubber gasket) that seals the bowl to the flange, the pipe fitting bolted to the floor. If the ring compresses, dries out or is not centered, water seeps out around the base. The sign is moisture on the floor after a flush, a faint sewer smell, or a stained ceiling below.

The test is to wipe the base dry, flush, and watch where water first appears. Water from the tank bolts or supply line is a different leak. Water on the floor at the base means the seal is suspect.

Replacing the ring is quick, but the flange and floor must be checked first. A cracked flange or soft subfloor will cause the new ring to fail, so the plumber looks at both before setting the toilet.

Running toilets, phantom flushes and a high water bill

A running toilet can waste a large amount of water without any noise. The easiest check is food coloring in the tank, then waiting a few minutes without flushing. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper is leaking.

Chlorine in the public supply gradually hardens rubber parts, so flappers are one of the most common wear items. Braintree's water report lists a free chlorine residual, not chloramine, so rubber parts wear in the usual way, and replacing the flapper every few years is reasonable. If the water in the bowl keeps rippling or the tank refills at odd times, the flapper or fill valve is the first suspect.

If you have fixed the toilet and the water meter still moves with everything off, the leak is elsewhere. See leak detection for how that is found.

How a toilet repair visit goes

The sequence keeps the repair short and prevents repeat visits.

1

Check the shutoff and supply.

The plumber confirms the stop valve closes and the supply line is sound, because every later step depends on a dry work area.

2

Identify the failed part.

Dye test for the flapper, a look at the fill valve, a rock test for the flange and a check for a hidden crack in the bowl.

3

Repair or reset.

Parts inside the tank are replaced with matching fittings. If the toilet is lifted, the flange is inspected and a new seal is installed before resetting.

4

Test and clean up.

Several flushes, a check for drips at every joint and a wipe of the floor. You get the explanation of what failed and what to watch.

Clogs: plunger first, then the trap and the line

A flange-style plunger with a firm seal clears most toilet clogs. If the water drains slowly or the toilet backs up each time, the problem may be in the drain line and not the bowl. A single clogged toilet usually means the trap. When several fixtures are slow, the clog is farther down the line.

Toilet clogs have a few causes: too much paper, flushed wipes, toys and objects, or a partially blocked vent. A closet auger passes through the trap without scratching the porcelain, while chemical drain cleaners are not recommended in a toilet.

If more than one fixture is affected, our drain cleaning page covers how branch and main lines are cleared. Repeated backups from every drain in the house can point to the main sewer, and a sewer line cleaning visit is the next step.

Repair the toilet you have or replace it

Replacement is not always better, but sometimes repairs cost more than they save.

Repair makes sense when.

  • The bowl and tank are not cracked.
  • The problem is one worn part, such as a flapper, fill valve or wax ring.
  • The toilet flushes well and you like how it works.
  • Repairs have been occasional, not frequent.

Replace when.

  • The porcelain is cracked or the tank leaks through the shell.
  • Parts are hard to match on an old model.
  • It clogs often even after the line is clear.
  • You are remodeling and want a different height, bowl shape or flush performance.

Installing a new toilet

A new toilet is a half-day job in most bathrooms. The plumber shuts off the water, removes the old unit, cleans the flange, sets a new seal and bolts the toilet down. Then the supply line is connected and the toilet is tested.

Plumbing work generally requires a permit under state rules, so ask the plumber whether replacement in your house needs one. For bathroom layouts or moving a toilet, see plumbing installation.
